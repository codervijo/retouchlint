The calls are coming in faster: "What did you edit on this shot?" Agents want to know—and more importantly, they need to be able to prove what changed when regulators ask. New MLS rules and state regulations around photo transparency have put listing photo disclosure front and center, and the lack of a clear, documented process is creating risk for both photographers and agents. If you're editing dozens of shots weekly without a documented trail, you're exposed. This guide walks you through building a simple, defensible listing photo disclosure workflow that protects both you and your clients.
## Understanding the Disclosure Requirement
### Why Agents Care About Your Edits
Real estate agents are under increasing scrutiny. State regulations—including California AB 723—now require disclosure when a listing photo has been digitally altered. This isn't about colour correction or exposure, which AB 723 expressly excludes; it's about images where elements of the property were added, removed, or changed: furniture, fixtures, flooring, paint colour, landscaping, the facade, or things visible from the property such as a neighbouring rooftop or power line.
When an agent lists a property with edited photos and can't prove what was disclosed, they face compliance violations and potential disputes with buyers. You, as the photographer or media company, are often the first point of responsibility. Agents are now asking for clarity: *What changed? Can you prove it?*
### The Compliance Stakes
A disclosure mistake isn't a minor paperwork issue. MLS data practices, state law, and fair housing standards all intersect here. If a buyer later claims they were misled by edited photos and the agent has no documented disclosure trail, the consequences cascade:
- Agent faces MLS violations and complaint investigations
- Your reputation as a service provider is compromised
- Both parties lack a defensible record if disputes arise
The solution isn't legal complexity—it's *transparency and documentation*.
## Common Disclosure Mistakes Photographers Make
### Assuming "Standard Editing" Doesn't Need Disclosure
Color correction, exposure balancing, and white balance adjustments are routine. But where's the line? Many photographers assume minor adjustments don't require documentation. The problem: "minor" is subjective. An agent later questioned about your work has no way to defend what you did—or didn't—do.
### Documenting Edits Informally
A text file, a scattered note in an email, or a verbal conversation isn't defensible. If an agent is audited or a complaint is filed, informal notes won't hold up. You need a systematic record that shows:
- What was edited
- How significantly it was changed
- When the disclosure was made
- Proof the agent received and acknowledged it
### Treating Photo Disclosure As the Agent's Job
It's tempting to assume the agent should handle all disclosures. But you created the photos and made the edits. You're the authoritative source. If you don't provide clear, documented disclosure to the agent, they're left guessing—and that's when problems start.
## Building a Defensible Disclosure Workflow
### Step 1: Categorize Your Edits
Not every adjustment is a disclosure item, but you need a clear rule. Create a simple matrix:
**Automatic Disclosure Items:**
- Object removal (clutter, cars, people)
- Sky replacement or significant sky enhancement
- Structural reframing (perspective shift, removing walls or features)
- Significant brightness, contrast, or saturation changes that alter perceived property condition
- Furniture or staging elements added or removed
**Minor (Non-Disclosure) Items:**
- Color correction to match natural daylight
- Exposure balancing
- Basic cropping
- Dust or sensor artifact removal
- Subtle contrast/clarity adjustments that don't change perceived condition
Document this matrix and share it with your agent clients upfront. This sets expectations and protects you both.
### Step 2: Create an Edit Summary for Every Shoot
After editing, produce a one-page summary for each property listing. Include:
- Property address
- Photos edited (by photo number or room)
- Specific changes made (with edit category: object removal, sky enhancement, etc.)
- Brief description of why the change was made (improved visibility, staging representation, etc.)
- Your signature or company stamp
- Date the summary was created
This document is your audit trail. It's simple, professional, and immediately proves you took the disclosure seriously.
### Step 3: Provide Disclosure Before the Listing Goes Live
Don't hand off photos and hope. Deliver:
1. The edited photos
2. The edit summary
3. A brief cover note asking the agent to confirm receipt and understanding
Make it easy for agents to acknowledge. A simple "I've reviewed the photo edits and disclose any material changes per MLS guidelines" is enough. Save their response—this proves they were informed.
## Protecting Yourself and Your Clients
### Document Your Process
Your process itself is your defense. If you can show a systematic workflow—consistent edit categories, documented summaries, regular communication with agents—you're protected from vague accusations of hidden edits or misrepresentation.
### Educate Your Agent Clients
Many agents are confused about what real estate photo disclosure actually requires. Send them a simple one-pager: "What Photo Edits Need Disclosure?" Include your edit matrix and explain why you follow it. An educated client is a loyal client—and one less likely to face a compliance surprise.
### Stay Ahead of Regulation Changes
Real estate photo disclosure rules are evolving. MLS policies vary by region; states like California are tightening requirements. Subscribe to MLS updates and photography industry bulletins. When rules shift, update your matrix and notify your agent partners.
## Moving Forward
The shift toward transparency in real estate photo disclosure isn't a burden—it's an opportunity. Photographers and media companies that document their work clearly are building trust with agents and positioning themselves as compliance-aware partners.
A simple, documented workflow protects both you and your clients. Start with a clear edit matrix, add a one-page edit summary to every shoot, and confirm receipt with your agents. That's defensible, professional, and exactly what agents are asking for when they call with "What changed?"
