Over the past year, real estate agents have started asking photographers the same question: "What changed?" They're not being difficult. They're getting liability questions from brokers and MLS administrators who now have to verify that any alterations to listing photos comply with state disclosure rules. If you can't quickly show what the original image looked like—and prove you documented it—you're both exposed. Original image access isn't about perfectionism. It's about creating a defensible record that protects your business and your clients when disclosure requirements are ambiguous or when disputes surface.
## Why Original Image Access Matters (More Than You Might Think)
Real estate photo disclosure is no longer a "nice to have" conversation. States are tightening rules. MLSs are asking harder questions. Brokers are passing compliance risk downstream to photographers. That means you're now the bottleneck between an agent who wants to know what edits were made and a compliance team that needs proof.
When you have original image access—meaning you can retrieve the unedited image quickly—you have three things competitors without it don't:
1. **A clear audit trail.** You can show exactly what the original capture looked like, what was changed, and why. This matters for MLS inquiries, broker vetting, and (if necessary) disputes with agents about scope of edits.
2. **A simple yes/no to "substantial alteration" questions.** Instead of remembering what you edited six months ago, you side-by-side the original against the delivered version. No guessing.
3. **Defensibility under statute.** When disclosure rules require you to document "material changes," having the original on file means you can back that up with evidence, not memory.
Without original image access, you're relying on editing software history files, email backups, or memory. All three fail at scale. If you're editing 20–50 shoots per month, that's fragile.
## What California AB 723 Actually Requires
California AB 723 requires two things whenever a digitally altered image is used to advertise a property: a conspicuous statement that the image was altered, and a link, URL, or QR code to a publicly accessible copy of the original, unaltered image, clearly identified as such. The second requirement is the one photographers underestimate. It means the originals cannot simply sit on your drive — they have to be reachable at an address someone else can open. Brokers and agents carry the liability, and they will push the question back to you as the origin point.
The law itself doesn't mandate that photographers keep originals on file. But it does require that alterations be disclosed. That disclosure is only credible if you can back it up. An agent saying "the photographer edited the sky" without evidence is a guess. An agent saying "here's the original and here's the edited version, and we disclosed the sky edit in the MLS" is documented compliance.
This is why original image access has become table stakes in competitive markets: it's the fastest way to prove you're doing the disclosure work right, and it's the fastest way to show an MLS or broker that edits were intentional and disclosed, not hidden.
## Building an Original Image Archive That Protects You
Original image access doesn't mean keeping every single RAW file forever. It means having a system where you can retrieve the *delivered* version of any original for any shoot, and compare it against what you edited.
**Start with what you deliver to clients.** Your delivered JPEGs *are* your originals from the MLS perspective. Those are the images agents will use as baseline reference if anyone asks "what was changed?" Keep copies of every delivered JPEG before you hand them off. Store them in a folder structure organized by shoot date and client name.
**Automate the save.** Don't rely on manual naming or folder creation. Build it into your workflow:
- Before you begin any editing session, copy the raw/master files into a read-only "originals" archive.
- After you finish editing, save one copy of the final JPEGs to the same location with a consistent naming pattern (date + client + image number works well).
- Use your editing software's scripting or batch features to make this routine. Most modern tools have hooks for pre/post-processing automation.
**Use cloud backup for redundancy.** Original image access only works if you can actually access the images when someone asks. If originals live only on an external drive in your office, a drive failure or accidental deletion becomes your problem. Use cloud storage (or a second local drive synced to cloud) so originals are retrievable even if your main system fails.
**Organize for speed.** When an agent or MLS asks "what changed in this image?", you need to find the original within minutes, not hours. Use a naming convention that lets you search and retrieve by client name, date, or shoot ID. Make it simple: `YYYY-MM-DD_ClientName_001_original.jpg` and `YYYY-MM-DD_ClientName_001_edited.jpg` in the same folder. You can then pull both into editing software side-by-side in seconds.
## Making Documentation Automatic
The best systems are the ones you don't have to think about. Build original image access into your delivery workflow so it happens every time, without exception.
**Create a checklist inside your editing software.** Before you start a new shoot, add a step: "Copy original files to archive." Before you finish, add: "Save final JPEGs to archive alongside originals." This takes 30 seconds per shoot and eliminates "I forgot."
**Use version control in your editing software.** Most modern tools (Lightroom, Capture One, Luminar) have built-in version history. Use it. Your software's version stack is not an archive—you need a file-based backup outside the software—but it does give you a record of which edits you applied and when. That's useful if you ever need to explain your editing decisions.
**Track changes briefly.** You don't need a 500-word edit log. But a one-line note per shoot helps: "Sky brightened, clarity increased, white balance warmed." This is useful context when someone asks questions months later.
## Getting Started
You don't need expensive software or a redesign of your entire workflow. Start small:
1. **This month:** Identify one shoot. Save the original JPEGs and the edited JPEGs into a folder labeled with the date and client name. Test retrieval. Does it take you 30 seconds or 5 minutes to find the original?
2. **Next month:** Repeat for all shoots. You'll develop a rhythm. Once you've done this for three months, you'll have a baseline archive for your recent work.
3. **Then:** Automate. Add a step to your shooting template or your editing software that does the save for you. One task per shoot, takes 30 seconds, becomes invisible.
4. **Ongoing:** When an agent or MLS asks what changed, you'll have the evidence. You'll be the photographer who can answer instantly, which matters in competitive markets and during disputes.
Original image access is how you turn disclosure compliance from a liability into a competitive advantage. You're not just protecting yourself—you're protecting agents who trust you, and you're building a reputation as the photographer who takes compliance seriously.
