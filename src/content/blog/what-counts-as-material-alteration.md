You've spent the morning editing a listing shoot—correcting white balance, removing a wine glass left on a counter, brightening shadows in the dining room. By afternoon, your agent client asks: *"Are any of these edits disclosure issues?"* If you're working in California or other states rolling out new photo-disclosure rules, this question is no longer hypothetical. Understanding what constitutes a material alteration isn't just about compliance; it's about having a defensible answer the next time an agent, buyer's advocate, or MLS asks what changed. This guide cuts through the confusion with practical distinctions that protect both your workflow and your credibility.

## AB 723 and the "Material Alteration" Standard

"Material alteration" is the phrase everyone in the industry uses, but it is worth being precise: California AB 723 does not use a materiality test at all. It turns on whether an image is *digitally altered*, which it defines as adding, removing, or changing elements such as fixtures, furniture, flooring, paint colour, landscaping, the facade, or features outside of and visible from the property. Everything else — lighting, sharpening, white balance, colour correction, angle, straightening, cropping, exposure, and comparable common adjustments that don't change how the property is represented — is expressly excluded. Not every edit is a disclosure issue, and knowing exactly where that line falls separates photographers who move confidently from those second-guessing every slider.

The law doesn't ban editing. Professional photographers have always corrected exposure, white balance, and composition, and AB 723 leaves all of that alone. What it requires is transparency about the other category: if you changed what is physically in the frame, the image needs a conspicuous "altered" statement and a link to the original. This distinction matters because it means your editing judgment still counts—you're not forbidden from standard post-processing, but you are responsible for knowing which edits cross from "enhancement" to "material change."

For photographers and small media companies serving agents, this creates an immediate practical problem: *How do you track which edits are material, prove what you did, and sleep at night?* The statute gives the framework; your workflow gives you the proof.

## Examples That Clarify the Line

The clearest way to understand material alterations is to see where they start—and where they don't.

### Edits that are typically *not* disclosable material alterations:
- Color correction and white balance (fixing a bluish cast from overcast sky; warming a room lit by cool fixtures)
- Exposure adjustment (brightening underexposed shadows; recovering blown highlights)
- Straightening horizons and perspective correction
- Removing small, temporary objects (coffee cup on a shelf; outdoor chair left in driveway)
- Sharpening and clarity adjustments
- Cropping for composition (as long as you're not removing a major feature like a visible neighbor's property line)

These fall into the category of *enhancement*—they're what professional photography looks like. A buyer expecting listing photos to be raw camera files is expecting the wrong standard; they expect the home to look its best when professionally photographed.

### Edits that *are* typically material alterations:
- Removing or significantly reducing structural damage (a large crack, water stain, or rot)
- Adding architectural features that weren't there (filling in a missing window to make it look symmetrical; cloning a fireplace into a wall)
- Dramatically changing wall or trim color (lightening a dark hallway so much that it changes perceived room dimensions)
- Removing or hiding major fixtures (a visible HVAC unit, satellite dish, or fence)
- Changing sky entirely (swapping an overcast sky for a blue one from a different time)
- Digitally staging (placing furniture, decor, or vehicles that aren't physically present)

These alter the factual representation of what a buyer would see on the property. The test: *Would the change influence a reasonable buyer's perception of the property's condition, layout, or features?* If yes, it's material.

The gray zone exists—a darkened room with a moderately brightened exposure, a wall with a color-cast correction that shifts tone slightly—and your judgment as a professional photographer is relevant here. The key is being able to articulate your reasoning and having a documented record of what you did.

## Building Your Audit Trail

One reason photographers are anxious about material alterations is that they often have no defense. You edit quickly, hand over processed files, and have no record of what you changed from the original capture. When a question arises months later, you're left explaining from memory.

A defensible audit trail doesn't require expensive software or legal overhead. It requires three habits:

**1. Keep your RAW files.** Your RAW capture is the baseline. If a photo disclosure question arises, you can show the before-and-after, making your editorial decision transparent. Many photographers already do this; if you don't, start. Storage is cheap; disputes are expensive.

**2. Document non-standard edits in a simple checklist.** If you make an edit that's outside routine color and exposure correction, note it. Not with narrative—just a line item: *"Brightened shadow detail in master bedroom +1.5 stops" or "Removed coffee cup, counter staging."* This takes 30 seconds per photo and becomes your defense if anyone asks.

**3. Share a summary with the agent client.** Before handing off processed files, send a one-paragraph note: *"I corrected white balance and exposure throughout the shoot, removed temporary objects in a few shots (coffee cup in kitchen, lawn chair in yard), and straightened horizons. No structural features were altered."* This isn't legal language—it's transparency. It prevents the agent from later claiming they didn't know about your edits, and it sets expectations upfront.

## Workflow Protection: The Simple System

You don't need new software; you need a consistent process. Here's the minimum-viable system that photographers actually use:

1. **Shoot in RAW + JPEG.** Your RAW is your evidence; your JPEG is your working file.
2. **Make your edits in a non-destructive editor** (Lightroom, Capture One, or similar). Adjustment layers and presets become your edit history.
3. **Before export, add one line to a job spreadsheet:** the client name, shoot date, and any material edits made (or "Standard color correction only" if none). This takes 10 seconds and covers you completely.
4. **Export to a folder** labeled with the client name and date. Archive RAWs separately.
5. **Deliver a simple photo manifest** to the agent: a text file listing which photos received special attention and why.

This process doesn't slow you down. It doesn't add cost. It gives you—and your agent clients—a defensible record that real estate photo disclosure compliance is built on your professional judgment, not luck.

## When in Doubt, Disclose

The safest legal posture is also the most practical: if an edit might be material, flag it. You're not admitting wrongdoing; you're being transparent. An agent who knows you brightened a dark room appreciates the honesty. An agent surprised by that disclosure later does not.

As state photo-disclosure rules evolve and agent questions multiply, photographers who move fastest are those who document their work. AB 723 and similar statutes don't eliminate your ability to edit professionally. They require you to know where the line is—and to prove, when asked, that you didn't cross it.

Your audit trail is your credibility. Build it as you work, and the next time an agent asks what changed, you'll have a clear, defensible answer.
